    Why industrial symbiosis, and why India?

    Kirchherr et al. counted 114 definitions of "circular economy" [13]. Most share one idea: keep materials in use. Industrial symbiosis is the business-to-business version, where separate firms swap materials, energy, water and by-products to gain a shared advantage from being close together [1].

    The Ellen MacArthur Foundation argues that India, still building most of its future industrial base, can design circularity in rather than retrofit it [14]. India also has something Europe lacks. Its informal recyclers, the kabadiwalas, already recover a large share of recyclables at very low cost [12].

    The Indian evidence: Nanjangud, Karnataka

    Bain, Shenoy, Ashton and Chertow studied an industrial area near Mysuru. They found firms already exchanging bagasse, ash and other residues, mostly through informal, market-driven deals rather than any planned eco-park [6]. This mirrors Chertow's point that symbiosis is often already there, waiting to be "uncovered" [3]. The opportunity in India is to make these hidden exchanges visible, then add the ones that are missing.

    What the app borrows from each

    • Kalundborg: the network map. Anchor tenants such as power, sugar and steel sit at the centre [2].
    • NISP: the facilitation slider. Brokered programmes turn far more potential exchanges into real ones [4].
    • Ulsan: the missing-link finder, which works like a feasibility pipeline for new partners [8].
    • Kawasaki: the city nodes. Municipal food waste and PET flow into industry [9].
    • Brazil: the kabadi layer. Informal collectors are treated as infrastructure and counted as livelihoods [11].

      How the numbers are made

      This is an exploration tool, not a feasibility study. Every number is an indicative, order-of-magnitude estimate meant to show where loops might close and roughly how much they matter.

      The matching engine

      1. Each cluster is a list of industry types, with a unit count and a scale factor.
      2. Each industry has typical waste outputs (t/yr per unit) and a list of streams it can accept as input.
      3. For every waste a node produces, the engine finds every other node in the cluster that accepts it. Some industries can also reuse their own waste, such as ceramic grog going back into tiles.
      4. Matched tonnes = supply × uptake rate, split among receivers in proportion to their size.
      5. The uptake rate is the facilitation level slider. It runs from 10% (firms find each other by chance) to 90% (an active broker like the UK's NISP).
      6. With the kabadi layer on, streams usually handled by informal aggregators (PET, cotton waste, husk, chrome shavings, cooking oil and so on) route through an aggregator hub. Their uptake gets a +15 percentage-point boost, capped at 95%. This reflects evidence that informal networks raise recovery rates in developing countries [12].
      7. Waste with no receiver in the cluster is flagged as a missing link, along with the industries that could absorb it.

      The metrics

      • Circularity = matched tonnes ÷ total waste generated in the cluster.
      • Value unlocked = Σ tonnes × (material value to receiver + disposal cost avoided by generator), shown in ₹ crore per year.
      • CO₂ avoided = Σ tonnes × substitution factor (virgin material or fossil fuel displaced).
      • Livelihoods = Σ tonnes × handling intensity (jobs per 1,000 t/yr in collection, sorting and transport).

      Known limits

      • Distance is ignored inside a cluster. Steam and heat really only travel a few kilometres, while ash and husk move hundreds.
      • Receiver capacity isn't capped, so a single brick maker can't really absorb a power plant's ash.
      • Cluster compositions are rounded and illustrative. They sketch each cluster's shape rather than count its firms.
      • Prices and factors vary a lot by region, season and quality. Treat them as starting points to replace with local data.
